Part 1: Masses and springs

I constructed an evenly spaced grid of point masses in row-major order and marked the pinned masses using the coordinates provided by the scene. I then connected the masses with structural springs between immediate horizontal and vertical neighbors, shearing springs between diagonal neighbors, and bending springs between masses two positions apart horizontally or vertically.

The screenshots below show the wireframe of scene/pinned2.json with three different combinations of constraints.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

I first calculated the external forces on each point mass, then added the spring forces using Hooke's law. I used a smaller spring constant for bending springs, multiplying ks by 0.2. Next, I updated the unpinned masses using damped Verlet integration. Finally, I corrected springs stretched beyond 110% of their rest length. If both endpoints were free, I split the correction between them; if one was pinned, I moved only the other endpoint.

I tested the parameters using pinned2.json, resetting the cloth between trials and changing one parameter at a time. The default values were ks = 5000, density = 15, and damping = 0.2%.

Spring constant

With ks = 500, the cloth was loose and wobbled more as it fell. Once it settled, the top edge sagged farther between the pinned corners. With ks = 50000, the cloth stayed more taut and held together as a sheet during the fall, with less deformation. It also had less sag at rest. The default value of 5000 felt somewhere between the two. Increasing ks makes the springs resist stretching and compression more strongly.

Density

Changing density reminded me of changing ks, but in the opposite direction. The lower density of 1.5 gave the cloth a more taut appearance, while the higher density of 150 made it look looser. At rest, the lower-density cloth had a shallower sag and held a more rectangular shape. During the fall, the higher-density cloth also looked looser and wobbled more, while the lower-density cloth held together more tightly. These changes followed the same loose-versus-taut pattern I noticed when testing spring stiffness.

Compared with the default density of 15, lowering density reduces the weight supported by the springs, while raising it increases that weight. Since ks stays the same, the heavier cloth stretches the springs further.

Damping

At 0% damping, the cloth continued to bounce and swing after falling. The motion did not seem to die out during the time I observed it. With 1% damping, there was very little bouncing, but the fall itself was much slower, almost as if the cloth were floating. Compared with the default 0.2%, removing damping allowed more continued motion, while increasing it to 1% strongly slowed the movement.

Damping reduces the movement carried over from the previous timestep. This explains why it reduces oscillations, but also why a high value can make the whole descent look slow.
