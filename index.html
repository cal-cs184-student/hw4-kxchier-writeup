Overview

In this assignment, I implemented a cloth simulator using a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs. I used Verlet integration to update the cloth's motion, handled collisions with spheres and planes, and used spatial hashing for self-collisions. I also implemented shaders for lighting, texture mapping, bump and displacement mapping, and environment-mapped reflections. Testing different spring stiffness, density, and damping values helped me understand how these parameters affect the cloth's movement and folds. I found the comparison between bump and displacement mapping particularly interesting because similar surface details could be created through changes to either shading normals or the actual geometry.

Part 1: Masses and springs

I constructed an evenly spaced grid of point masses in row-major order and marked the pinned masses using the coordinates provided by the scene. I then connected the masses with structural springs between immediate horizontal and vertical neighbors, shearing springs between diagonal neighbors, and bending springs between masses two positions apart horizontally or vertically.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

I first calculated the external forces on each point mass, then added the spring forces using Hooke's law. I used a smaller spring constant for bending springs, multiplying ks by 0.2. Next, I updated the unpinned masses using damped Verlet integration. Finally, I corrected springs stretched beyond 110% of their rest length. If both endpoints were free, I split the correction between them; if one was pinned, I moved only the other endpoint.

I tested the parameters using pinned2.json, resetting the cloth between trials and changing one parameter at a time. The default values were ks = 5000, density = 15, and damping = 0.2%.

Spring constant

With ks = 500, the cloth was loose and wobbled more as it fell. Once it settled, the top edge sagged farther between the pinned corners. With ks = 50000, the cloth stayed more taut and held together as a sheet during the fall, with less deformation. It also had less sag at rest. The default value of 5000 felt somewhere between the two. Increasing ks makes the springs resist stretching and compression more strongly.

Density

Changing density reminded me of changing ks, but in the opposite direction. The lower density of 1.5 gave the cloth a more taut appearance, while the higher density of 150 made it look looser. At rest, the lower-density cloth had a shallower sag and held a more rectangular shape. During the fall, the higher-density cloth also looked looser and wobbled more, while the lower-density cloth held together more tightly. These changes followed the same loose-versus-taut pattern I noticed when testing spring stiffness.

Compared with the default density of 15, lowering density reduces the weight supported by the springs, while raising it increases that weight. Since ks stays the same, the heavier cloth stretches the springs further.

Damping

At 0% damping, the cloth continued to bounce and swing after falling. The motion did not seem to die out during the time I observed it. With 1% damping, there was very little bouncing, but the fall itself was much slower, almost as if the cloth were floating. Compared with the default 0.2%, removing damping allowed more continued motion, while increasing it to 1% strongly slowed the movement.

Damping reduces the movement carried over from the previous timestep. This explains why it reduces oscillations, but also why a high value can make the whole descent look slow.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Collision Implementation

For sphere collisions, I checked whether a point mass was inside the sphere by comparing its distance from the center to the radius. If it was inside, I found the point on the sphere's surface in the direction from the center to the mass. I calculated a correction from the mass's previous position to this surface point, then scaled it by (1 - friction) before updating the position.

For plane collisions, I calculated the signed distances of the mass's current and previous positions using dot products with the plane's normal. If the mass crossed the plane, I projected its current position onto the surface. I then added a small SURFACE_OFFSET toward its original side of the plane so it would not sit directly on the surface. I applied the correction from its previous position, again scaling by (1 - friction).

In the simulation loop, I checked every unpinned point mass against each collision object after the Verlet update and before correcting overstretched springs.

Cloth Draped Over a Sphere

I tested sphere.json with ks = 500, 5000, and 50000, resetting the cloth between trials. I kept density at 15 and damping at 0.2%.

With ks = 500, the cloth draped closely around the sphere and formed narrower folds along the hanging edges. At the default ks = 5000, the sides spread out a little more. With ks = 50000, the cloth had a wider shape and broader folds, looking less loosely draped around the sphere. The stiffer springs resist deformation more strongly, so the cloth holds its shape as it hangs over the surface.

Cloth Resting on a Plane

In plane.json, the cloth fell onto the plane and settled flat on its surface rather than passing through it. I used the default parameters: ks = 5000, density = 15, and damping = 0.2%.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation

I used spatial hashing to avoid checking every possible pair of point masses. I divided space into boxes with dimensions 3 * width / num_width_points, 3 * height / num_height_points, and the maximum of those two values. I used floor to find each mass's box coordinates, then combined the coordinates into a hash key. At each timestep, I rebuilt a map from these keys to lists of point mass pointers.

For each unpinned mass, I checked the other masses in its bucket, skipping itself. If another mass was closer than twice the cloth's thickness, I calculated a correction pointing away from it. I averaged the corrections and divided by simulation_steps before updating the position. This keeps each correction small when several masses are close together.

Falling and Folding

In selfCollision.json, the bottom of the cloth reached the plane first and began folding as the rest fell into it. The cloth then collected into a stack of folds. Later, it spread out into a much flatter shape. The screenshots below show these three stages using ks = 5000, density = 15, and damping = 0.2%.

Effects of Density

At density = 150, the cloth gathered into many small wrinkles near the bottom as it folded onto itself. At density = 1.5, the folds were broader and smoother. This reminded me of the stiffness comparison: increasing the mass while keeping the same springs made the cloth look more easily deformed. I kept ks = 5000 and damping = 0.2% for both trials.

Effects of Spring Stiffness

With ks = 500, the cloth crumpled into smaller folds where it reached the plane. With ks = 50000, it formed larger, smoother folds and resisted the small wrinkles seen with the weaker springs. Increasing ks also increases the stiffness of the bending springs, so the cloth resists tight bends more strongly. I kept density = 15 and damping = 0.2% for both trials.

Part 5: Shaders

Shader Programs

A shader is a program that runs on the GPU. The vertex shader processes each vertex and calculates its screen position. It also passes along information such as position, normal, and UV coordinates. These values are interpolated across each triangle, and the fragment shader uses them to calculate the color at each fragment.

Blinn-Phong Shading

I combined ambient, diffuse, and specular lighting. Ambient adds a constant base brightness. Diffuse lighting depends on how directly the surface faces the light, which makes the cloth's folds visible. Specular lighting adds highlights based on the alignment between the normal and the halfway vector between the light and viewing directions. I used an ambient coefficient of 0.1, a specular coefficient of 0.5, and a shininess exponent of 8.

With only ambient lighting, the cloth looked like a flat silhouette. Diffuse lighting revealed its curved surface and folds. The specular-only image was mostly dark, with highlights in a few areas. Combining the terms gave the cloth a brighter appearance while keeping the variation across its folds.

Texture Mapping

I sampled the texture using the interpolated UV coordinates and used the sampled color as the fragment color. I chose my watercolor artwork as the custom texture. The flowers and stems wrapped around the top of the sphere and followed the hanging folds of the cloth. This shader displays the image directly, without adding lighting.

Bump and Displacement Mapping

For bump mapping, I sampled neighboring heights from the texture to estimate changes in the U and V directions. I used these differences to tilt the normal, then transformed it into world space using the tangent, bitangent, and normal basis. I used the resulting normal in the lighting calculation.

Displacement mapping also moves each vertex along its normal using the sampled height. I then used the same fragment shader as bump mapping to shade the displaced surface.

I used a black-and-white floral pattern as the height texture for both methods, with Normal = 20 and Height = 0.03. The white background received more outward displacement than the dark flowers. With bump mapping, the flowers look engraved, but the cloth's outline stayed smooth. With displacement, the surface and outline changed. The sharp transitions in the image created jagged areas, especially on the cloth. Initially, the displaced cloth looked overly spiky with Height = 0.1. Switching back to Phong showed that the underlying cloth simulation still looked normal, which helped me identify displacement as the cause. Lowering Height reduced how far the vertices moved and made the surface detail more controlled. I settled on Height = 0.03 for the comparison screenshots.

Height texture: Black floral pattern on white background with repeating flowers and leaves , from Vecteezy.

Sphere Resolution Comparison

I compared sphere resolutions of 16 by 16 and 128 by 128, keeping the height texture and scaling parameters the same. The results looked fairly similar from a distance. Bump mapping showed detailed flowers at both resolutions because the normal adjustments happen per fragment, although the lower-resolution sphere still had a more angular outline.

The difference was more noticeable with displacement. At the lower resolution, the geometry formed broader, angular changes. The higher resolution followed more of the floral boundaries, but the sharp edges of the height image still produced jagged transitions.

Environment-Mapped Reflections

For the mirror shader, I reflected the camera-to-surface direction around the surface normal, then sampled the cubemap in that reflected direction. The sphere showed a curved reflection of the environment, while the cloth's folds stretched and bent the reflected image. Rotating the camera changed which parts of the environment appeared on the surfaces.
